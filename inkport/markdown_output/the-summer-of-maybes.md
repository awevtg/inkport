# the summer of maybes

*By samayra — Wed, 01 Jul 2026 11:08:20 GMT*

**Tags:** `family`, `memories`, `stories`, `writing`, `this-happened-to-me`

When I first started any of my blog sites, every bio had the line: 18-year-old in the summer before college. It was July of last year when I opened Medium, and October when I started my Substack, but the bio remained the same. I was neither eighteen (I still had a few months to go) nor was it summer. That line was my way of naming the summer I was waiting for.

I wanted to believe that by the time that eighteen-year-old summer ended, I would have ended up where I wanted to be.

I spent a lot of time this month doing things I had always wanted to do. Some of them had been sitting on lists I made years ago, after watching my sister prepare to leave for college.

Now we are both in this borrowed time. She has ten days left before joining her new — old — job after months of internships, and I am making another list of the things I want to do when I get a job. With an age gap like ours, my mother believes the list will continue until our deaths.

I am the youngest in my family. My parents say they don’t, but they already have plans for the things they want to do when I ultimately leave this Garden of Eden for whatever conquest awaits me.

For the past few years, my life has mostly been school, home, and examination centres. There were brief exceptions — a summer spent travelling to Delhi for workshops, weekends with circuits and robots, moments that made the world feel much bigger than the room where I studied.

Somewhere during that time, I realised what I wanted to spend my life doing. I just didn’t know that all the summers ahead would slowly stop feeling like summers and become just seasons instead.

That season took a toll on me. I kept adding more work, more expectations, convinced that doing more would eventually become enough. Sometimes it worked. Sometimes the results were worse than I had imagined.

Eventually, my parents asked me to stop everything else I was doing and focus on one goal. The story, cut short, is that I went, gave the exam, and came back home.

The only feeling I remember, sitting at my desk that night, was emptiness. I don’t know if I had ever felt that empty in my entire life until then. It was a heavy quietness, and I promised myself that I would never willingly put myself in a situation that would make me feel that way again.

You can put yourself in shoes bigger than your feet, but eventually you begin to notice the bruises. Sometimes that takes miles. Sometimes it takes years.

My father had a childhood full of stories. I don’t know whether it is the way he describes his life through words, or my imagination shaped by old black-and-white Bollywood films and the Hindi literature I inherited through my sister.

One of the stories takes place when he was still in school. Near the school, there was a garden of litchis, guarded by a watchman with a watchful eye. It was widely believed that whoever tried to steal from it would be caught and beaten.

My father and his friends used to make small dents in the wall every day so they could climb it. This continued for several days until one day they decided that one of them would finally climb over.

“It was obvious that the one with the least brains in the group would climb the wall,” my father had said. “At that time, I was the one.”

On his first try, he couldn’t do it. The next day, encouraged by his friends, he tried again.

The plan was simple. He would climb the wall, reach the tree, shake a branch, let a few litchis fall outside the boundary, and climb back.

What they didn’t expect was how much the tree would move. As he climbed, the branch closest to the wall swung away. His friends ran. Even my father’s books were left behind. He was made to sit beneath the tree for two hours as punishment. When he returned home, his friends were waiting for him.

“What happened?” they asked.

“The watchman beat me a lot,” he told them.

Whenever he tells the story now, he ends it the same way.

“I never shared my homework with them again. I was still in the group, but never with them in the same way. I started studying more after that. Even if someone had explained the importance of friendship to me ten years later, I wouldn’t have understood it — or trusted it.”

I think about that story often now. We all inherit something from our parents — sometimes values, sometimes fears, sometimes unfinished lessons. My father came home from that garden with fewer friends than he had that morning. I came home from an examination hall with fewer certainties than I had when I entered.

Maybe every summer before adulthood asks for something different in return. Mine simply asked me to let go of the version of my life I had already written.

I think we all tell ourselves stories before adulthood. My parents’ was about the life they would have after I left home. My father’s was about the friends waiting for him outside the litchi garden.

None of those stories turned out exactly as they were imagined, but all of them changed the people who had believed them.
